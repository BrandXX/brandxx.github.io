How a regex joke became a design principle

The story behind Defiantly Not Regex

It started with a question I kept returning to while working with models and harnesses in my TechSoft Systems AI lab: What makes intelligence dependable?

The year of the harness

Bigger models can do more. More agents can attempt more work. Neither automatically makes a system trustworthy. Useful intelligence needs structure around it: a way to understand the request, choose appropriate tools, define boundaries, demand evidence, check results, and recover when the first approach fails.

I came to think of this as the year of the harness. In the original lab, Hermes provided the agent environment and Open-WebUI the front end. PIPER connected intent to task contracts, freshness and evidence requirements, specialist routing, and runtime guards. EVA handled orchestration and synthesis.

This was more than classifying a prompt. I was exploring how to make the requirements of a request explicit and preserve them as work moved through the system. Routing, validation, and recovery were engineering responsibilities, not things to leave entirely to a model's improvisation.

The distinction was simple: capability tells you what a model might do; system design and testing establish what you can rely on.

The personal connection

Then the same distinction became personal.

After COVID, I noticed that under fatigue, my words did not always arrive as cleanly as the thoughts behind them. I could know exactly what I wanted to say and still transpose letters, lose a word, or watch autocorrect confidently substitute the wrong one. Sometimes the idea was clear while the output was noisy.

As an engineer, I found myself reaching for an analogy: a working core, a communication channel that sometimes degraded under load, and a need for better checks before sending a message. Proofreading, rest, rephrasing, and clarification were useful recovery mechanisms.

It is an analogy for my experience, not a clinical explanation or a claim that people are machines. In fact, it led me to the opposite conclusion:

A flawed output should not be mistaken for a flawed mind.

Why "defiantly"?

The phrase arrived as a small programmer joke:

DEFIANTLY NOT REGEX
Parse carefully. Humans are not strings.

The difference between definitely and defiantly is intentional. "Definitely" declares certainty. "Defiantly" pushes back against the wrong abstraction: the temptation to treat a person, a conversation, or an ambiguous request as nothing more than a pattern to match.

Regex was the metaphor, not the villain. It does its job extremely well. Trouble starts when we ask it, or any rigid mechanism, to answer a question about meaning that it was never built to answer.

The artifact became an experiment

I turned the phrase into a black-and-white magnet and signature graphic, with fine print carrying an encoded explanation. The website adds a rollover between the original image and a clearer reconstruction.

The small Base64 line exposed a weakness. In an early version, individual characters were too ambiguous for dependable extraction. Context made the purpose of the message understandable, but it did not magically restore the precise original bytes.

That forced a better workflow: identify the intended message from the available context, return to a known source, render the text deterministically, and validate the encoding. The improved image could then carry the message more reliably, with supporting context in other forms.

The irony was too good to ignore. A joke about parsing had become a demonstration of why literal parsing sometimes needs help, and why that help must still be checked.

What stayed with me

The experience connected three ideas I had previously considered separately: robust AI requires a harness; communication requires context; and people deserve to be understood beyond the imperfections in their words.

It also established an important boundary. Context can help recover intent, but it is not permission to invent facts or silently rewrite someone's meaning. When exactness matters, verify. When meaning is uncertain, ask.

An insight from the lab had become a personal reminder, a joke, and a design rule I intend to keep using:

Blurry tokens. Clear meaning.
Parse carefully. Humans are not strings.
